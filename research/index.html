Paper Airplanes How planes fly, and how to read a fold sheet

💭 What I was thinking (round 1)

The sheet is the plane. A small computer model folds the paper step by step, finds where each fold line crosses the top layer, and prints it exactly there, on whichever side is facing up at that moment. Follow the numbers and the lines are always in the right place, even after the paper has been folded and turned over.

That only works if the page prints at exactly 100% and the back lines up with the front, so every sheet has a 2-inch scale check and ⚙️ has an alignment test you hold up to a window.

The goal is careful folding in person: sharp creases, corners that meet exactly, both wings the same. A simulator can wait.

📐 Reading a fold sheet
Valley fold (fold in, toward you). The paper makes a V like a valley. Mountain fold (fold out, away from you). The paper makes a peak like a mountain. Edge of the paper. Arrow with a filled head: fold toward you. Arrow with a hollow head: fold away from you (behind). Step number. Do them in order.

This is the standard way origami books draw folds. It goes back to the Japanese paper folder Akira Yoshizawa (1911–2005), whose diagrams in the 1950s were later written up as a shared system by Samuel Randlett and Robert Harbin. Learn it once and you can read almost any folding book.

🌬️ The four forces

Lift holds the plane up: the wing is tilted a little nose-up, so it pushes air down and the air pushes the wing up. Big wings push on more air, which is why the wide-wing glider floats.

Weight pulls it down. Thrust is your throw; a paper plane has no engine, so after the throw it is always slowly coming down: gliding. Drag is the air rubbing and pushing back; sharp creases and a pointy nose make less of it.

⚖️ The balance point

Balance the plane on one fingertip under the body. It should balance a little in front of the middle of the wings. That's why so many folds stack paper at the nose.

Too far back: the nose pops up, it stalls and drops. Too far forward: it dives. A paper clip on the nose moves the balance forward.

🕰️ A little history

Leonardo da Vinci (1452–1519) filled notebooks with drawings of flying machines and of birds' wings. None of his machines flew, but he studied how air moves.

George Cayley (1773–1857), an English engineer, was the first to describe the forces on a wing: lift, weight, drag and thrust. In 1853 his glider carried a person across a small valley.

Wilbur and Orville Wright tested gliders on the sand dunes at Kitty Hawk from 1900 to 1902, learning to balance and steer before they added an engine in 1903. Folding and throwing a paper glider is the same kind of testing.
